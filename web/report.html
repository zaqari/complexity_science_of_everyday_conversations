Dataset consolidation, statistical analyses, and data visualizations by @ZP Rosen (6/24/2026)
The way conversation partners work together to generate new semantic information explains how conversation facilitates distributed cognition

Project Overview

Description/Goals

When people work together, we're often way better than the sum of our parts [26],[6],[12],[24],[40]. And we accomplish that via... drum roll please... conversing with eachother [8],[7],[2].

But what are you actually doing when you say something (anything) in a conversation with folks? What does talking really do?

To get at this, you kinda have to understand what utterances (the things you say) are actually made of, first. Every utterance you make is actually, when you zoom in on it, comprised of a collection of related ideas and concepts [20],[37],[25]. What you say isn't really one big meaning. It's a bunch of smaller interconnected pointers to ideas in your head that you hope your partner in a conversation will pick up on and piggyback off of. And, straight up, when we're conversing with folks in the real world, what we're looking for is very much how well our partners can pick up on what we're laying down, take parts of it, and keep the conversation going by adding their own twist on those ideas [21],[37].

The thing of it is this: when you say something and your conversation partner (CP) builds on those ideas it has some really important effects on a conversation. Every utterance changes what's relevant in the here and now of a conversation [23],[34],[5] -- what's in yours and your partner's common ground at that particular moment. Conversation unfolds by iteratively adding on top of old pieces of common ground. And those pieces are the smaller, interrelated ideas from those prior utterances.

This causes, in actuality, a kind of movement during conversation. You move from one set of ideas and concepts to a new set of ideas. The movement from one set of things to a new set of things generates information (in the statistical sense [35],[33],[1] ). The greater the difference between the ideas in some later utterance (we'll call the later utterance $y$) and the ideas expressed in the earlier utterance ($x$), the more information you added. Thinking about information in this way is important, because it links information back to a kind of intellectual work people do when interacting with eachother. And it takes more "work" (and thus more information) to move from one set of ideas to other ones, especially when they're particularly unrelated to what's been said before. You've probably felt this yourself in real life when someone changed topics in a conversation and you weren't expecting it!

Thinking about how conversation progress, then, from the perspective of information being generated: if you randomly pick any utterance in a conversation (call it the utterance $x$), that utterance contains a set of ideas and concepts that the speaker wanted folks to be aware of. Both the person who spoke the utterance $x$ and their partners will then make new utterances, keeping the conversation going. Each utterance generates a certain amount of new information relative to the utterance $x$ -- every utterance moves the conversation a little farther away from the ideas in $x$ than the last utterance. And this adds up as time goes on.

And there's one more thing to consider here: this process is fundamentally collaborative. Because, while each speaker and partner brings their own ideas to bear on the conversation -- you might be responding to the same set of ideas, but you have your own ideas about those ideas -- when you're talking with one another, every utterance that's made (1) updates the state of the conversation as previously mentioned, and (2) is fair game for every speaker and partner to take pieces of and build from moving forward.

This process of adding information and updating conversation is more important than you might think. Being able to add new information together is front and center in a number of diverse domains of experience you engage in every day. Working with someone to learn a new thing? Collaborative learning hinges on students and instructors co-building the set of ideas the student needs together [11]. Want to maintain a pleasant conversation? Well, adding information together and adhering to the norms of how we do that is an important consideration for making that possible [14]. Are you working with people to solve a complex problem? Then it's extremely important that you and your partners build on eachother's ideas well via having god-tier communication with one another [7],[32],[31],[30],[4],[42].

Each of these is an example of what some researchers have called embodied, extended, or distributed cognition [12],[24],[6]. But I personally like the term "collective cognition" for these kinds of processes [38],[39] -- the process of people coordinating with one another to co-construct some group-level cognitive outcome.

Okay okay. So let's link some of these things together now! Conversation is driven forward by people continuously generating information relative to some prior state of the conversation. They do this collaboratively, and every person who says something contributes to the changes to the current state of the conversation. Relative to whatever point we started from, each turn adds a little more to the total information that either the same speaker, or one of their partners, is going to contribute over the course of the conversation. So our little "moves" away from the ideas expressed in our starting point (*in foreboding tone* utterance $x$!) add up. And somehow -- some-freaking-how! -- doing this allows us to do collective cognition. While all these points make sense, there's a problem here: There actually isn't a good formal description of how this process works in conversation! We don't have a quantitative model of it. And so, while we can see the outcomes of collective cognition as listed above, we actually don't have a good roadmap for how we get there! What are the rules of conversation that allow us to go from building on prior ideas together to full-blown collective cognition??

This is also worth considering because if we can get a map that tells us how we go from the more basic behavior of building on old ideas (together) to complex coordinated behavior, this can actually help us unify a number of conflicting theories, too! For example, Interactive Alignment Theory (IAT: [29],[18] ) predicts that just the act of talking with people causes y'all to align on linguistic features and thus, eventually, entire understandings of yours and your CPs' worlds. It's a very cool thought. But work in Communication Accommodation Theory (CAT: [36],[19] ) actually shows that alignment like that is modulated by your feelings towards CPs, and other work on Interpersonal Synergy Theory (IST: [16],[17],[15],[10] ) shows that it's not really that people simply align with one another so much as they form compensatory pacts of related behaviors. But wait, it's even more complicated than that. Because it turns out that there is a periodic ebb and flow in the entailments of sentences that are squished right up next to eachother (entailments here meaning what sentences in aggregate imply about the world, not the composite ideas and concepts inside of them on their own) during conversation too [27],[28].

If all of these accounts of how conversation ought to work are predicting radically different things, which one is accurate? Which one captures reality the best? It might actually be all of them. The theories above all deal with much higher-level phenomena than how we add new ideas to our conversational idea pot. And a good framework for how we do this lower-level work can help us unify these different accounts by showing how we can get to each of them from the same set of starting blocks.

So, I'm not going to say that complex dynamical systems (CDS) science holds all of the answers to these questions... but I'm not about to say that it's not getting us in that direction either, y'all!

CDS is the study of, well, complex systems. Many of those systems are composed of myriad smaller sub-systems that work together in interesting ways [9]. Some of those systems can do something called distributed computation. That's where, by dint of how the pieces of that system work together, they fuse into what you might think of as a single kind of computer -- one where parts of the computations the computer performs are distributed to the various different parts that the computer is made of. In terms of conversation, this would be like saying "when people talk with eachother in a group, they form one computational unit that generates new ideas, and the work to do that is offloaded across all the people in the group".

Distributed computational systems can (and do) arise naturally in nature (and in engineering, too...). But they have certain characteristics when they do:

(1) Distributed computational systems exhibit power-law, or "fractal" scaling in their outputs [41],[22]. What the heck does that mean??? For these systems, it means that if you start by generating, say, 1 bit of new information in your first turn, then in your next turn you might generate 1.5 bits of new information (that gets added to your ledger). So on and so forth. But when you add the information generated across turns all up, you eventually hit a kind of ceiling effect (you can see that actually, in figure (1c) in the methods section!). That ceiling effect is like the curl in a fractal pattern, pulling current outputs ever inwards towards prior outputs. This is actually a kind of memory in the system, because even if you start from, say, 10 bits of new information, the way the system progresses from there behaves exactly the same as it did when you only produced 1 bit of information. The initial (or even the recently prior) state informs what happens to the next state, and thus the system "remembers" what happened before (or, at least, what it does next is informed by prior states/information). This allows you to do computations on any kind of input, without the whole system breaking [13],[3],[41].

There is another reason why fractals are important for computational processes too, and it's somewhat related to that ceiling effect. Fractals are complicated. No, I mean really complicated. They're so complicated in fact that if you tried to zoom in on any part of a fractal pattern, you could keep on zooming in on it and get the same shape back, every. Single. Zooming. Step. Because of this, fractals run into this thing called the "halting problem" -- where a computer is capable of running any kind of program, simply because if left to its own devices it could feasibly run forever [43]. Fractally speaking, you actually can't zoom in on part of a fractal and get to some bottom-most, maximum amount of Zoom where the shape disappears, and so if you told a computer to do that it would never "halt" or stop. What's cool about this is that you can store an infinite amount of things using this shape. If you assign a letter, for example, to some point in a fractal pattern, you could keep zooming in on that same point and assign infinite letters to the smaller and smaller portion of the fractal. And if there's a specific order to those letters, too, then what ends up happening is that you can store an infinite number of relationships/steps to get from one letter to the next this way. Ergo, fractals can store infinite amounts of information [13],[3]. If you have a starting point, you can make infinite, albeit increasingly smaller, changes to that starting point. Infinite computation!

The second thing that distributed computational systems need to have is (2) demonstrable proof that what the system as a whole is doing is because all the pieces are actually connected [22]. Because if the system is just a bunch of parts doing their own thing, then that's not distributed computation. That's just a bunch of noisy computers.

So then. Let's put this in with the other pieces we have so far. Conversation works by people working to collaboratively update the state of a conversation. Doing this generates information that adds up over time. We need to get from that to something that can allow collective cognition to happen. Collective cognition is in fact a special case of distributed computation, where disparate parts come together to form a single computational system (brains, unite!). If we want to move from building on eachother's ideas to collective cognition, then, the way we generate and add information to conversation should have the two defining features of distributed cognition: (1) power-law scaling in how information compounds over time, and (2) proof that the way information compounding arises from the interaction of the pieces of the system itself (i.e., the speakers engaging in that conversation).

I don't think it's much of a spoiler alert, but that's precisely what we found. Analyzing 5,448 conversations (in 9 different languages in 3 different language families) using a language-model enabled, information theoretic framework (see our methods) we found that indeed that if you pick any random starting point in a conversation, relative to that point (1) speakers and their conversation partners both generate fractally distributed outputs, and (2) that the behavior of speakers when they do this is well-coordinated.

So what's the link between how we build on prior ideas and collective cognition, folks? Answer: the way we build on prior ideas is the computational scaffolding that enables collective cognition in the first place.

Methods

There are two tasks we needed to accomplish for this project. (1) measuring how much information is generated when you compare two utterances to one another (like comparing an older utterance to a later utterance), and (2) measuring how much the amount of information changes when comparing two utterances to eachother depending on how far apart the two are in time (i.e. how much new information gets added from one utterance to the next, when we keep comparing new utterances to the semantic content/ideas in an older utterance that other speakers are building on top of).

Convergence Entropy/Information

A good method for answering question one is Convergence-Entropy as described in [29]. I'm about to hit you with some math, but here's how it works at a high level to start. First, you take an earlier utterance ($x$) and an utterance spoken after it ($y$). You convert all the tokens/words (we label each one using $i$ for the $i^{th}$ token) in the utterance $x$ into what are called token embeddings ($E_{xi}$) using a language model. You do the same think for all the tokens ($j$ -- same logic) in the utterance $y$. In this study, we used a large language model (LLM) to generate embeddings, and research shows that these embeddings are actually really good representations of the semantic meaning of words (even when compared to actual human semantic processing, [16, 18, 24, 33]). This entire process is captured in equation 1.

(1)$$E_{xi} = LLM(i \in x)$$

Next, you calculate how close the closest token $j$ in the utterance $y$ is to each token $i$ in the utterance $x$. You can then convert that distance to a probability using some fancy statistics (a Half-Gaussian, with mean 0, meaning that if the distance between the closes token $j$ in $y$ to a token $i$ in $x$ is 0, then the two tokens mean the same thing). See equation

(2)$$P_{xi}(E_y) = P_{\mathcal{N}_{[0,\infty]}} \left( \min_j \bigg(CoE(E_{xi}, E_{yj}) \bigg) \bigg| \mu=0, \sigma \right)$$

If you have a probability, you can calculate information content (thank you Claude Shannon [31]!). Information content is how much extra information you'd need in order to get the meaning of the token $i$ in $x$ in this case. See equation 3.

(3) $$I(x;y) = -\sum_{i \in x} \log P_{xi}(E_y)$$

What you're left with is how much semantic information is generated (how much extra statistical work you'd have to do) to get from one set of embeddings to another!

Quick note to the wise here: the equation above is for information, not the normalized entropy. And in other places this is reffered to as convergence-information (CI) not convergence-entropy (CE).

It's important to note that the scaling factor $\sigma$ in equation 2 is arbitrary. Especially if you're calculating information content like in equation 3. How big or small $\sigma$ is will quantitatively change how big or small the difference in probability is for different distance measurements, but it won't change that there is a difference. It monotonically scales based on $\sigma$. So $\sigma$ changes how sensitive your measurement is, but it won't change the existence of differences, and so long as you're using the same $\sigma$ for all measurement, it means that all those differences will at least be in the same scale, which is important for comparing across different examples and time.

Some extra, cool nerdy shit

Technically, equation 3 is not entropy. It's what's called the Shannon information. But it has a really important relationship with entropy proper.

Shannon defined entropy as the average, expected information for any measurement taken in a system. He also used the letter $H$ to symbolize entropy. Go figure. Even so, that means entropy is by definition this:

(4) $$H_x \doteq \lim_{n_{i \in x} \rightarrow \infty} \frac{I(x;y)}{n_{i \in x}}$$

But that also means that any measurement of information can be expressed as expected information/entropy plus some error ($\epsilon$)

(5) $$I(x;y) = \mathbb{E}[I(x;y) ] + \epsilon$$

or, more precisely as

(6) $$I(x;y) = (H_x * n_{i \in x}) + \epsilon$$

Hold onto that last point for a minute. Trust me.


Integrating Total Information Contribution by Speakers and partners

CE (or CI, really...) gives us a solid mechanism for measuring how much information is required to recover the ideas in some utterance $x$ from the ideas expressed in a later utterance $y$. But speakers don't just dump information in a single utterance. They keep generating information. Over and over again. And over again. And over again and over... you get the picture.

Speakers and their partners, in subsequent utterances $y$ will keep generating new information. And it's interesting to ask what is the dynamical nature ("dynamical" meaning how something changes from one point to the next in time) of the way they do this? Do people slowly generate information after some prior turn $x$ and then leap off into the sunset in later utterances adding tons of new information rapidly after a period of relative little information generation? Do they linearly add new information, like gentlefolks, building up new ideas in a straight line over time? Or do people's information generation behaviors exhibit self-similarity or fractal patterns, where, because there is a kind of attractor to prior ideas, information is generated rapidly, starting from the lowest possible amount of information early on and hitting a ceiling later?

Two things are needd to answer these questions. First, we need a way to measure how much information gets added earlier versus later in subsequent uttterances. Second, we need a way of calculating how the rate of that information changes.

On its own, CI gives us a raw information measurement. But we still need a way to measure how that information compounds turn over turn. Enter integral calculus. Yes. I'm going to retraumatize you with integrals. Deal with it. If we take the integral (which is really just a fancy sum) for every utterance (up to 200 utterances into the future in this study) that a speaker make after the utterance $x$, we can ask what percentage of that integral has been "filled" after every utterance a speaker contributes. Cool. That gives us how much information has acrued, and the math looks like:

$$CDF(\Delta_t) = \frac{\int_{i>1}^{\Delta_t} I(x;y_{\Delta_t}) \partial (\Delta_t)}{\int_{i>1}^{\Delta_t \in T} I(x;y_{\Delta_t}) \partial (\Delta_t)}$$

Where CDF is the cumulative density function -- that's just what this kind of integral divided by the total integral is called in fancy statistics speak.

Now, we can tell which of the three behaviors I rattled off before by calculating a power-law exponent for how much the CDF changes as a function of changes in time $\Delta_t$. The power-law exponent for a whole dataset of CDFs can tell us how some value behaves over time. More on that in a second after I show y'all the equation. What's nice, too, is that we can calculate a power-law exponent via the following regression equation (which we can apply to all of our data)

$$\log CDF(\Delta_t) \sim \beta_0 + \alpha \log \Delta_t$$

Which can be "exponentiated" to look like

$$CDF(\Delta_t) \sim e^{\beta_0}\Delta_t^\alpha$$

and the value $\alpha$ is our power-law exponent describing the scaling/dynamical behavior of the CDF depending on temporal distance $\Delta_t$. Nifty!

Here's where this gets really useful. When $\alpha \approx 0$, this means that information accumulates chaotically. What does that mean? It means some of the jumps in the density of information, early on, are much higher than you'd expect even compared to later additions logarithmically, and the opposite is true too. Picture a completely random squiggly line on a piece of paper. That's what $\alpha \approx 0$ indicates in terms of behavior. Conversely, $|\!|\alpha|\!| \approx 1$ is a different kind of randomness -- Gaussian randomness. In this case, each turn adds about the same amount of information, and thus we can say when this is true that information adds up linearly and (seemingly) orderly over time. Neat! When $|\!|\alpha|\!| > 1$, that means information starts of by accruing slowly and then shoots to the moon as time goes on. And when $|\!|\alpha|\!| < 1$, that means we have something that at least has self-similar tendencies if not outright fractal scaling. Information starts low, jumps up quick, and hits a ceiling/constraint.

One measurement, but excellent resolution into different kinds of possible dynamical behaviors!

Mutual influence/complexity matching

An important part of Langton's definition of what makes a complex system capable of distributed computation across its parts is demonstrating that the pieces of that system are influencing eachother's behavior. Otherwise, there's no proof that they're ultimately working together.

It's hard to show that power-law scaling (and thus fractal information generation) are related between the parts of a system. Langton is a bit of a cheater, here, because all the systems he studied were simulated -- he could literally write into the program itself "this part talks to this part, make it so!" But in the real world, we need actual proof that the pieces are talking. And even more specifically, we need to show in our case that power-law scaling in one part of the system affects the power-law scaling in another part of that system.

Enter the idea of complexity matching [21]. Complexity matching is the idea that entire distributions of behavior influence in part of a system influence the distributions on behavior in the other part. It's a comparison thus between two distributions. When complexity matching is 0, it means the two systems are synchronized in their behavior. Because it means there is no difference in how the two distributions themselves behave. But systematic differences are still useful and important here. Because when two systems have systematic differences in behaviors (like the systems always being some value like 2 apart), then they're actually synchronized, and one demonstrably influences the other.

This definition was important to Mahmoodi because Mahmoodi was interested in coordinated behavior in very different systems or between radically different kinds of things. Think: measuring how the behavior of waggling your fingers influences the behavior of tapping your toes. That's why he focused on differences in whole distributions (because fingers and toes don't move the same, but how they move relative to what affordances in movement they have can become influenced by one another). Other folks though have used complexity matching in ways like tracking how people manage rate of speaking and onset times [1], though, amongst a whole fleet of other things.

For us, we test whether the difference between speakers' power-law distributed rates of information generation relative to their own prior utterances and their conversation partners' rates of information generation systematically differ or are the same. This actually is easy to do in the current study, given that it's a comparison between two clearly different, but potentially related behaviors, and we measure the difference in distributions using a paired t-test for this reason.

Testing for context sensitivity via null permutation testing

Complexity matching is good evidence of coordination, but it's likely not enough. We need to make sure that any power-law scaling effects we observe in the data arise because the speakers in the conversation are actually engaging with one another.

We need to make test that power-law exponents are context-sensitive--that if you take utterances out of a conversation and compare them to random utterances, you only see the observed effects when they are embedded in their own original context and not in random data.

One way of testing this is to create a null-permuted or null dataset by randomly pairing utterances from different conversations with one another, basically run all the steps you do for the real data on that randomly permuted dataset, and then compare the power-law exponents for the real data to the null data power-law exponents. If the power-law exponents in the observed, real data are context sensitive, then they should be less chaotic than what we get when looking at the null permuted data.

We implemented this by randomly sampling 10% of the data, and then swapping out the utterances $y$ in that 10% random sample with utterances from entirely different conversations. In the cross-linguistic study, we made sure that utterances $y$ were in the same language, but barred utterances being paired with other utterances from the same conversation.

We then compared the power-law exponents for the observed data against the power-law exponents calculated for the null permuted data using a Welch T-test (we wanted to make sure we controlled for unequal variances).
